An arpeggio is a chord played one note at a time instead of all at once. The word comes from the Italian arpeggiare, "to play like a harp", because a harpist naturally sweeps through a chord string by string.

Where arpeggios come from

An arpeggio uses exactly the notes of a chord, so it's built the same way: by stacking 3rds. Start on a root, skip a note of the scale, take the next, skip one, take the next. In C major that gives C, E, G. Add one more 3rd and you get a seventh chord: C, E, G, B.

The physics: notes are frequencies

A piano string vibrates back and forth a certain number of times per second. That rate is its frequency, measured in hertz (Hz), and it's what you hear as pitch. The tuning standard sets A above middle C at 440 Hz.

  • An octave doubles the frequency. A4 is 440 Hz, A5 is 880 Hz, A3 is 220 Hz. Your ear hears pitch on a ratio scale, not a linear one, which is why every octave feels like the same distance even though each spans twice as many hertz.
  • Each half step multiplies by the same amount: the 12th root of 2, about 1.0595. Twelve half steps multiply by exactly 2: one octave. The formula for any key is f = 440 × 2(n − 69)/12, where n is its MIDI note number (middle C = 60). This system is called 12-tone equal temperament.

The harmonic series: chords hidden inside one note

A vibrating string doesn't vibrate at just one frequency. It also vibrates in halves, thirds, quarters and so on, producing quieter overtones at 2, 3, 4, 5, 6… times the main frequency. Together they're the harmonic series, and their mix is a big part of why a piano sounds different from a flute playing the same note.

Listen to the first eight harmonics of a low C. Harmonics 4, 5 and 6 spell out C–E–G: a major triad is built into the sound of a single note.

Why some notes sound good together

  • EvidenceOvertones line up. When two notes are in a simple ratio, many of their overtones land on the same frequencies and reinforce each other. In C–G (3 : 2), G's 2nd harmonic matches C's 3rd. When overtones land close but not on top of each other, they interfere and produce beating: a wobble in loudness. Hermann von Helmholtz described this in 1863. Plomp and Levelt (1965) showed that roughness peaks when two tones sit within about a quarter of the ear's "critical band".
  • EvidenceThe brain likes harmonic-sounding mixtures. Listeners' preference for consonant chords tracks how closely the chord's combined frequencies resemble one harmonic series (McDermott, Lehr and Oxenham, 2010).
  • EvidenceCulture matters too. The Tsimane' people of the Bolivian Amazon, with little exposure to Western music, rated consonant and dissonant chords as about equally pleasant (McDermott et al., 2016). The physics creates the contrast; listening experience shapes whether we like it.

Equal temperament: a compromise you can hear

Pure ratios can't all fit on a keyboard where every key works in every key signature. Equal temperament spreads the error evenly. Fifths are almost perfect, only 2 cents flat (a cent is 1/100 of a half step). Major 3rds are about 14 cents sharp. On C4–E4, E's 4th harmonic (1318.5 Hz) sits next to C's 5th harmonic (1308.1 Hz), so they beat about 10 times per second. A pure 5 : 4 third has no beating.

EvidenceReal piano strings are stiff, so their overtones run slightly sharp of the ideal series (inharmonicity). Tuners compensate by tuning high notes a little sharp and low notes a little flat, called stretch tuning.

Why an arpeggio still sounds like a chord

  • EvidenceThe ear groups notes into patterns. Notes close in time and drawn from one harmony are heard as a single "stream" that implies a chord, even when no two sound together (Bregman's auditory scene analysis).
  • The notes overlap anyway. A piano note keeps ringing after the next one starts, especially with the sustain pedal, so an arpeggio often builds into a full chord.
  • ConventionMotion plus harmony. An arpeggio gives a chord movement and rhythm. It's both melody and accompaniment at once, which is why it's everywhere in piano writing.

History and uses

  • Baroque: composers wrote "broken chords" for keyboards that couldn't sustain. Bach's Prelude in C (1722) is almost nothing but arpeggios.
  • Classical: the Alberti bass (low–high–middle–high), named after Domenico Alberti, keeps the left hand moving under a melody.
  • Romantic: sweeping arpeggios across the whole keyboard. Beethoven's "Moonlight" Sonata (1801) opens with slow triplet arpeggios; Chopin's Étude Op. 10 No. 1 stretches them over four octaves.
  • Pop and film: arpeggiated accompaniment in ballads, and repeating arpeggio patterns (ostinatos) in film and minimalist music.
  • Jazz and improvisation: playing a chord's tones as a line over that chord ("outlining the changes") is one of the first improvising skills.

Technique

  • The thumb crossing is the hard part. Going up in the right hand, the thumb passes under the hand to the next group. Going down, a finger crosses over the thumb. The left hand mirrors this. Prepare the thumb early so the crossing doesn't cause a bump.
  • Move the arm, not just the fingers. Arpeggios span more than one hand position, so the forearm carries the hand along the keyboard while the wrist shifts slightly sideways. Gripping or stretching the fingers to reach causes tension and uneven notes.
  • Rotate the forearm slightly toward the next note. Many teachers describe it like turning a doorknob a little with each group.
  • Stay relaxed and even. Speed comes from relaxed, even motion, never from force.

Fingering

Standard fingerings follow a few rules: the thumb goes on a white key, the hand repeats the same group each octave, the outermost note uses finger 5, and a wider gap (a 4th) gets a longer reach.

How to practice them

  1. Block it first. Play the chord shape as a solid chord, then break it. Your hand learns the shape as one unit. EvidenceGrouping notes into larger chunks is how skilled players reduce what they need to remember.
  2. Learn the shape in Wait mode, hands separately, saying the chord tones aloud.
  3. Add the metronome slowly in Tempo mode. Aim for even timing before speed. The Evenness score measures it.
  4. Vary the rhythm: long–short, short–long, accents on different notes. EvidencePracticing variations of a movement builds a more flexible, durable skill than repeating one version.
  5. Hands together once each hand is secure.
  6. Go around the circle of fifths with the 12 keys option, so every key gets practiced, not just the easy ones.

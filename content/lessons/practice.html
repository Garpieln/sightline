The goal of practice is to change your nervous system, not to log minutes. These findings shape every tool in Sightline.

What the research says

  • EvidenceFix errors at their exact location. Strong pianist learners stop at the precise spot of a mistake, repair it slowly, and keep wrong versions from repeating. Practice time alone predicted little (Duke, Simmons & Cash, 2009).
  • EvidenceSleep consolidates motor skills. Finger-sequence skills improve overnight without more practice (Walker et al., 2002). Short, frequent sessions give more consolidation cycles than one long one.
  • EvidenceMixing beats blocking for retention. Rotating between pieces and skills feels harder in the moment and produces better long-term memory (contextual interference).
  • EvidenceRecall strengthens memory. Playing a passage from memory, or naming notes before checking, builds memory more than re-reading (retrieval practice).
  • EvidenceMental practice works. Imagining playing produced motor-cortex changes and real gains, though less than physical practice (Pascual-Leone et al., 1995).
  • EvidenceConstant feedback becomes a crutch. Feedback after every attempt helps during practice but weakens retention. Fading it over time builds independence. That's why Read & play has a "Summary only" setting.
  • EvidenceFocus on the sound, not the fingers. Attention on the result (an external focus) generally improves motor performance over attention on body movements.

Turning that into a session

  1. Warm up on the skill of the week (scale, five-finger pattern).
  2. Spend your freshest minutes on new material.
  3. Sight-read something new, once, without stopping.
  4. Improvise or compose with what you just learned.
  5. Review older pieces from memory.
  6. Record one take and write down one fix.
Try itNext time a passage breaks, don't restart from the top. Find the exact note, play it with one note on each side slowly three times correctly, then put it back in the phrase.

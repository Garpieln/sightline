Producers and composers link musical choices to feelings. Some links are supported by research. Many are learned conventions from film, pop and classical traditions, and a few are folklore. They're tagged below.

Mode and harmony

  • EvidenceMajor sounds happier, minor sadder to Western listeners, from childhood on. The strength of this link depends partly on cultural exposure.
  • EvidenceTempo and register matter as much as mode. Fast and high reads as happy or excited; slow and low reads as sad or serious. A fast minor piece can sound energetic, not sad.
  • EvidenceDissonance reads as tension, and its resolution as release. Expectation and surprise are major drivers of the "chills" response to music.

Do specific keys have emotions?

Opinion18th-century writers such as Schubart described key "characters" (for example, E♭ as heroic, D major as triumphant). Those ideas came from older unequal tunings, where each key really did sound different. On a modern equally tempered piano, every major key has identical interval ratios. What still differs is register (pitch height), which notes fall on black keys (affects hand shape and feel), and associations you've built from music you know.
